How to Spot Online Scams Targeting Nigerian Businesses (2026 Guide)

Published by Rayglobal Digital World • November 8, 2026 • 10 min read

If you run a business in Nigeria, you are a target. The Nigeria Inter-Bank Settlement System (NIBSS) reported that fraud losses in the Nigerian banking sector reached ₦42.6 billion in 2025, up from ₦17.67 billion the previous year[1]. Businesses, not banks, often bear the loss.

The scammers are sophisticated, persistent, and constantly updating their methods. This guide covers the 7 most common scams targeting Nigerian SMEs, how to spot each one, and how to protect your business.

Part 1: The 7 Most Common Scams

1.1 The Fake Bank Alert

How it works: A "buyer" shows you a forged credit alert or SMS, and you release goods before the money actually clears. The money never arrives[2].

How to protect: Never trust an SMS or a screenshot. Open your bank's official app and confirm the credit balance has actually increased. If it hasn't, the payment is fake. This single habit will save you more money than any other security measure.

The advanced version: Scammers can spoof the SMS sender ID to match your bank's name, making the fake alert appear in the same thread as your real bank messages. The app is the only source of truth.

1.2 The Overpayment Scam

How it works: A "customer" sends you ₦50,000 for a ₦20,000 item, then asks you to refund the ₦30,000 difference. The original payment was fake or reversed. You lose ₦30,000[3].

How to protect: Only refund after the money has fully cleared and settled in your account which usually takes at least 24 hours. If someone asks for an immediate refund, it's a red flag.

1.3 The Urgency Scam

How it works: Scammers create panic. "I need this today." "Pay now or lose the slot." Legitimate businesses do not pressure you into irreversible actions.

How to protect: Slow down. Verify. Ask questions. If someone won't let you think, that's not a customer, it's a scammer.

1.4 The OTP Request

How it works: Someone impersonates your bank, a delivery company, or a customer. They claim there's a problem with your account or a transaction and need your OTP to "fix it."

How to protect: No bank will ever ask for your OTP over the phone. No legitimate client will ask for your card details to "send you money." If anyone asks, it's a scam. The Central Bank of Nigeria has repeatedly warned that fraudsters impersonate bank officials to extract OTPs[4].

1.5 Phishing Links

How it works: Scammers impersonate banks, government agencies (like CAC or FIRS), and even courier services. They send a link that looks legitimate but leads to a fake login page.

How to protect: Before clicking a link in an SMS or email, hover over it to see the actual URL. If it looks wrong (e.g., cac-registration.ng instead of cac.gov.ng), it's a phishing site. Also check for HTTPS (though scammers are now buying certificates, so it's not a perfect signal).

1.6 The "Account Compromise" Call

How it works: A caller claims your account has been compromised and you need to "verify" your details. They ask for your BVN, card details, or account number.

How to protect: Hang up and call your bank directly using the number on their official website (not the number the caller gives you). Never give personal details to an inbound caller.

1.7 The Invoice Fraud

How it works: A scammer sends you an invoice that looks like it's from a supplier. They change the bank account details. If you pay, the money goes to them, not the real supplier.

How to protect: Always verify a new bank account directly with the supplier using a phone number you already have on file. Never trust new bank details sent by email alone.

Part 2: The 5 Golden Rules

Rule #1: Verify Inside Your Banking App

Never trust an SMS, a screenshot, or a caller. Open your bank's official app and confirm the balance. The app is the only source of truth.

Rule #2: Be Suspicious of Urgency

Legitimate businesses do not pressure you. If someone does, it's a red flag. Slow down and verify.

Rule #3: Protect Your OTPs and PINs

No bank will ever ask for your OTP over the phone. No legitimate client will ask for your card details to send you money. If anyone asks, it's a scam.

Rule #4: Check for Phishing Links

Hover over every link before clicking. Check the actual URL. If it's wrong, don't click.

Rule #5: Verify New Bank Accounts Directly

If a supplier sends you new bank details, call them on a number you already have on file to confirm. Never trust new bank details sent by email alone.

Part 3: The Advanced Techniques

3.1 Two-Factor Authentication (2FA)

Enable 2FA on every account that supports it: email, banking, social media, WhatsApp. Use an authenticator app (Google Authenticator, Authy) instead of SMS 2FA where possible, SMS 2FA can be intercepted by SIM swap fraud.

3.2 Separate Email for Financial Accounts

Use a dedicated email address for banking and financial accounts. This makes it harder for scammers who compromise your main email to access your money.

3.3 Strong, Unique Passwords

Use a password manager (Bitwarden, 1Password, Dashlane) to generate and store unique passwords for every account. Never reuse passwords, if one site is breached, all your accounts are compromised.

3.4 Regular Account Audits

Once a month, review your bank statements, credit card statements, and online accounts for suspicious activity. Report anything you don't recognize immediately.

Part 4: What to Do If You've Been Scammed

4.1 Immediate Steps

  1. Contact your bank immediately: Call the fraud line, not the general customer service. Ask them to freeze the account or reverse the transaction if possible.
  2. Report to the police: File a report with the Nigeria Police Force Cybercrime Unit. This is required for any legal action.
  3. Report to NIBSS: File a complaint with NIBSS Fraud Desk.
  4. Report to EFCC: If the amount is significant, report to the Economic and Financial Crimes Commission.
  5. Change all passwords: If your email or bank account was compromised, change passwords for every account.

4.2 Recovery Realities

The reality is that recovery is rare. Most scams use accounts that are quickly closed, or money that is transferred out of Nigeria within minutes. Prevention is the only reliable protection.

Part 5: The Nigerian-Specific Scenarios

5.1 The "CAC Portal" Scam

With the new free CAC registration scheme, scammers have created fake portals that look like the official SMEDAN portal. They charge ₦5,000-₦20,000 for "registration" that should be free.

How to protect: Only use the official URL: portal.smedan.gov.ng. Never trust links sent by individuals on WhatsApp or social media.

5.2 The "Job Offer" Scam

Scammers post fake job listings on LinkedIn, Jobberman, and WhatsApp. They ask for a "processing fee" or "training fee" before the job starts. Legitimate employers never charge for jobs.

How to protect: Never pay money to get a job. Report fake listings immediately.

5.3 The "Investment" Scam

Scammers promise 30-50% monthly returns on investments. These are Ponzi schemes. They collapse, and everyone loses their money.

How to protect: Any investment promising guaranteed high returns is a scam. Legitimate investments have risk and modest returns.
